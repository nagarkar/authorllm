# unsourced-claims

Purpose: every borrowing has a source a reader could check, or a request for one.

- Flag: a quotation (an epigraph, or an unmarked close paraphrase of a famous line) with no work, no location, or for a translation no translator; a word's meaning or derivation in a classical or sacred language stated as fact; a doctrine attributed to a tradition or figure in a form its scholars would dispute; a strong characterization, in the author's voice, of a tradition's idea with no mainstream reading cited for it; a research position offered as settled science; a count or statistic stated as fact; a dating on which specialists differ; a historical claim a historian might dispute; a position attributed to a named author, above all a living one, with no work and location; "it has been argued" or the like hiding a source; a note that names no work and location.
- A sufficient source is a work and a location (section, verse, page, saying), and the translator for a translation. A bare title, a year or a name does not pass. A source anywhere in this essay, text or note, covers the passage; one in another essay does not.
- Common knowledge passes when the field's standard reference states it without citation and the passage uses it in that standard form.
- Repair: plant `[Footnote: <what a sufficient citation gives>]` after the sentence. Name a real work only when sure it exists. Never write a footnote, never place a bare anchor, never invent a citation. An unmarked borrowed line also gets its quotation marks or attribution in the text.

Leaves alone: the author's coinages (the glossary); a thinker's standing biography; a textbook result; a hypothetical number; a date every reference work gives; the declared thesis of a named work; work and location given in the running text. Whether a sourced claim is true is not this filter's question.
