# claim-strength

Purpose: each claim is worded as strongly as its status allows, no more and no less.

- Too strong: a verb of demonstration ("proves", "it follows", "confirms", "must") on an axiom, definition, analogy or conjecture; a tendency stated as a law ("eventually", "sooner or later", "inescapable"); a run of bare present-tense sentences reporting the essay's own conjecture as observed history; a claim about the present age stated as settled with nothing behind it; a model introduced with no sentence saying it is a model.
- Too weak: an event or established fact hedged with "could" or "may". Restore the plain past or present.
- Repairs, in this order; take the first that works: (1) name the condition or mechanism and keep the claim definite; one governing clause at the head of a run, or one sentence where the model is introduced, can cover every sentence under it; (2) narrow the scope ("many religions", "a generation that inherits without testing"); (3) a verb that carries the status ("argues", "suggests", "proposes") or a modal that still asserts ("tends to"), with the reason in sight.
- Never a bare "can" or "may". Never hedge every verb in a run. Prefer owning a central conjecture to softening it.

Not a finding: a generic present under a frame still in force; a claim about one life; a valid step from the book's defined terms; questions or free indirect speech in another's voice; a typifying generic ("a child"); a cited report at its proper strength.

Owns every hedge, modal and verb of claiming; later filters leave them as they stand. Leaves: what a claim is about (`cross-references`, the letter), sources (`unsourced-claims`), a concession paragraph (the letter).
